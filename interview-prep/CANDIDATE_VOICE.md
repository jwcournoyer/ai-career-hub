# Candidate Voice Profile — Spoken-Content Style Reference

> **What this is:** A profile of how YOU actually talk, built from your real interview transcripts — so that AI-drafted spoken content (interview answers, recruiter messages, thank-you notes) sounds like you instead of like the AI that drafted it.
>
> **Why it exists:** AI assistants have a recognizable cadence — em-dash oppositions, parallel-structure framings ("Three reasons:"), numbered inline reasons, intensifiers like "actually" and "fundamentally." If you read a drafted answer aloud and it sounds like polished-LLM prose rather than how you talk, the draft fails in the room. This file teaches the AI your cadence and catalogs your personal failure modes so drafts correct for them.
>
> **A second reason this matters now:** most interviews are recorded by AI note-takers. The transcript — including every name you mispronounce, every number that drifts, every volunteered negative — becomes an artifact the rest of the panel reads. Your voice profile is also your transcript-hygiene tool.
>
> **When to consult:** Any time the AI is drafting content you will speak aloud or send to a human — interview answers, recruiter messages and replies, drafted emails, LinkedIn DMs, cover letter prose, mock-interview scripts, "what to say if asked" lines.
>
> **How to use:** Draft → run the Self-Check Rubric (Section 3) → revise where the rubric flags → deliver.
>
> **Where NOT to apply:** Internal prep docs, analysis sections, anti-pattern lists, tracker entries, scorecards. Those are for your private orientation — AI-cadence does no damage there. Don't over-correct where polish doesn't hurt.

---

## How to Build This File

This file starts as scaffolding. It becomes valuable as you fill it from real evidence:

1. **Collect transcripts.** Save every interview transcript and AI note-taker summary to `done/TRANSCRIPT_*.md`. Phone screens count. Mock interviews count.
2. **Extract your fingerprint** (Section 1). After 3-5 transcripts, ask your AI assistant to extract: your actual sentence openers, fillers, vocabulary, sentence shapes, and your strongest confident moments. Verbatim quotes only — what you actually said, not what you wish you'd said.
3. **Catalog your anti-patterns** (Section 2). From the same transcripts plus your debriefs, capture the moments that hurt — verbatim quote paired with a corrected version in your own voice. The categories below are pre-seeded because they are near-universal interview failure modes; your verbatim examples make them actionable.
4. **Keep it role-agnostic.** Use round-type descriptors ("R1 recruiter screen," "final-round panel," "peer-architect round") instead of company or interviewer names. Voice patterns are durable; specific opportunities are not. The dated transcripts in `done/` carry the provenance.
5. **Maintain it** (Section 7). After each debrief, add new anti-patterns, promote phrasings that landed well into Confirmed Positive Patterns, and refresh stale examples.

Until this file has real content, the AI should skip voice-check steps rather than guess at your voice.

### 1.3 Vocabulary you actually use

Three lists to extract:

## 2. Anti-Patterns with Paired Rewrites

Each category below is a near-universal interview failure mode. As your transcripts accumulate, fill each table with **verbatim quotes from your own transcripts** paired with **corrected versions in your own voice**. When drafting any speaking-part content, the AI scans for these shapes and rewrites.

The parent principle behind every category: **interviewers care about their problems and whether you can solve them — not about your problems.** Every answer wants the shape *their problem → my solution → my proof*, not *my history → my limitations → my hope to grow*.

### 2.1 Lead-with-the-gap reflex

Opening an answer with what you HAVEN'T done, what you're NOT, or what you're working on — before answering the actual question. Fires on capability probes ("What's your X depth?"). The fix: lead with the closest-analog real experience and the dimensions you can reason about, THEN bound the gap honestly in one clause, then close with how you'd ramp.

| Verbatim (transcript) | Corrected version |
|---|---|
| *"[your quote]"* ([round type]) | *"[lead with closest analog → bound the gap → commit to ramp]"* |

### 2.2 Self-deprecation where ownership is the right move

Honest self-deprecation ("I'm just a cog," "I'm not great at X") in moments where ownership is what the question wants. The deprecation may be authentic, but the rep cost is real.

### 2.3 Volunteered negatives about your own state

Disclosing unemployment, application volume, job-search frustration, or interview-process detail that nobody asked for. All of it creates asymmetry against you. Also includes personal-life disclosures the interviewer can't act on ("I have a sick kid home today") — cut them; don't make the interviewer manage emotions they didn't sign up for.

### 2.4 Volunteered negatives about previous employers

Even mild characterizations ("it's a startup that never grew up," "they had no roadmap") read as defensive — and the interviewer infers you'd talk the same way about them later. Build a **departure lock**: one neutral, rehearsed sentence for why you left each role, and never extend past it. Watch for the lock breaking under casual conversational cadence — it usually holds under direct probing and slips when you're relaxed.

### 2.5 Self-disqualifying mid-answer

Telling the interviewer, mid-delivery, that your own answer doesn't fit the question ("...so that wasn't really an example of X, I guess"). Worst of both worlds — an answer plus a disclaimer that the answer was wrong. Cut the disclaimer; let the answer stand, or pick a different story before you start.

### 2.6 Domain-dismissive framing

Dismissing a domain you worked in ("doesn't really matter what they did") to disclaim domain ignorance up front. Reframe: *"I learned [domain] from scratch as [role] — niche vertical, but the fundamentals translate."*

### 2.7 Underclaiming your own work

"I helped build" for things you designed. "Side project" for paying client work. "I just asked AI and it told me what to do" for work you architected and validated. The listener anchors on the junior framing. Promote the language to match the actual ownership level.

### 2.8 Resignation filler

"It is what it is," "that's how it goes, I guess" — signals defeat and low energy. Cut entirely; substitute silence or a pivot.

### 2.9 Disclosed compensation history

Prior salary disclosure is a one-way ratchet downward — recruiters reliably use it to set the ceiling. When asked about prior comp or flexibility, deflect to your current market target and stop. Don't justify the target with what you used to make.

### 2.10 Mid-call rate anchor collapse

You anchor your target early in a call, then accept a sub-anchor number later in the same call ("yeah, that's fine"). Once collapsed, the anchor is gone with that recruiter. Locked response when a below-anchor number surfaces: *"That's on the lower end of where I'm looking. If there's room to push closer to [target], that matches my target. I understand you'll advocate for what's available."*

### 2.11 End-of-call question fumble

You prepped questions but can't retrieve them under pressure. Fix: memorize a 2-question default shortlist that works for any round, and bring a physical card with the top 3 role-specific ones. Suggested defaults:

1. *"What does success look like at 90 days for this role from your seat?"*
2. *"Who's the team I'd be working most closely with?"*

### 2.12 Competing-offer over-disclosure

When asked about other opportunities in flight: don't name the company, don't name the title, don't name people, don't explain why the other role is worse. The locked abstract framing: *"Another offer at [stage], [one differentiating clause if needed] — this is the priority I'm working toward."* The interviewer needs the priority signal, not the comparison.

### 2.13 The mistake question

The reflex is minimization-via-denial ("I try not to make many mistakes") or contradictory framing ("I wouldn't call it a mistake, but..."). The question wants a demonstrated-learning rep. Four-beat lock:

- **Lead** with the mistake itself — own the frame.
- **Proof** with the specific decision and outcome.
- **Bound** with what the actual error was (not someone else's perception of it).
- **Close** with what changed in your playbook going forward.

Pick one reusable real mistake from your history and rehearse it into this shape.

### 2.14 Passive team-enablement answer

When the probe is "how would you raise the team's bar?", the weak reflex is the passive helper framing ("I'm happy to answer questions when they have them"). The probe is hunting for proactive enablement — standards, documentation, review cadences, pattern libraries. Build a 30/60/90 lock: *"30 days: [standard documented and enforced]. 60 days: [shared library / decision records]. 90 days: [review cadence where the team brings the design]. The goal is they produce better work because the standards are clear, not because I'm in every PR."*

## 3. Self-Check Rubric

Run before delivering any drafted speaking-part content. Each "yes" on items 1-13 means revise; item 14 is a positive check (yes is good).

1. **Solve-their-problem first?** Does the FIRST sentence answer their problem, not disclose yours? If it opens with your gap, employment state, or self-doubt — rewrite.
2. **Lead-with-the-gap reflex?** Did the answer open with what you haven't done instead of what you bring? Restructure: closest-analog case first, then bound the gap.
3. **Self-undercut close?** Does the LAST sentence land the answer or disqualify it ("...but I haven't really done that much of it")? Cut the disclaimer.
4. **Comp / job-state disclosure?** Prior salary, application volume, "not currently working," other rounds in flight? Cut all of it.
5. **Em-dashes as rhetorical breaks?** Almost nobody speaks in "X — Y" oppositions. Rewrite with "and," "but," "so," or a comma.
6. **Parallel-structure framing?** "Three reasons:", "Two things to hold:", "Not just X — Y". AI-cadence. Rewrite as natural conversational flow.
7. **Numbered inline reasons?** "First... Second... Third..." inside prose. Rewrite with your natural connectors.
8. **LLM-flavored intensifiers?** "actually," "specifically," "fundamentally," "Honestly," "essentially." Replace with YOUR intensifiers (Section 1.3).
9. **Domain-dismissive framing?** "Doesn't really matter what they did." Replace with learned-it-from-scratch framing.
10. **Underclaim of work?** "Helped" where "designed/built/owned" is accurate; "side hustle" where "client engagement" is accurate. Promote the language.
11. **Resignation filler?** "It is what it is." Cut.
12. **Self-deprecating tax inside an acceptance?** Accepting a stretch and then undercutting the acceptance. Cut the undercut; the acceptance stands clean.
13. **Real-name precision?** Generic phrases ("a previous client") where your real engagement names exist? Replace with the specific name.
14. **Confident closer present?** Does the answer end on a short declarative landing, or trail into hedging? End the sentence sooner.

The meta-test: **read the draft aloud.** If it sounds like how you talk when you're flowing well, it's right. If it sounds like the AI wrote it, run the rubric again.

## 4. Application Notes

### 4.1 Where this profile does NOT apply

Internal prep docs, candidate profile, analysis sections, scorecards, debrief notes, rubrics, tracker entries, architecture diagrams, technical specs. These are for your private orientation — AI-cadence is fine here.

### 4.2 Where it DOES apply

Interview answers (every round), mock-interview drafts, "what to say if asked" lines, recruiter replies, cover letters, LinkedIn messages and connection notes, career-narrative openers, rate negotiations, drafted emails to recruiters / hiring managers / references.

### 4.3 Spoken artifacts ≠ stylistic targets

Your transcripts include live-fluency stutters and restarts — real, authentic, and NOT what to put in a written draft. When capturing your voice in writing:

- ✅ Preserve rhythm, vocabulary, contractions, sentence length, connectors, confident closers
- ✅ Preserve a small amount of natural filler for texture
- ❌ Don't transcribe stutter-restarts or fluency stalls
- ❌ Don't write filler clusters into drafts — that's parodying your spoken voice, not writing in it

## 6. Probe-Shape Recognition

A meta-skill that develops across interview cycles: read the SHAPE of the probe before answering, then route to the matching framing. The same reflex (usually minimization or over-disclosure) fires under different probe shapes; the fix is recognizing which shape is in play.

### 6.1 Capability probe vs conflict-of-interest probe

If you have side work, a consulting entity, or a personal project, the same topic needs two different framings:

- **Capability probe** ("Tell me about your current work" / "How are you using X?") → strongest-engagement framing first; the soft bound (part-time, non-competing) goes at the END.
- **Conflict-of-interest probe** ("Would that be a conflict?" / "Are you comfortable with that?") → soften FIRST: part-time, bounded scope, non-competing, "if this role is the right fit, this role is the priority." Deploying the strong framing on a COI probe gives the interviewer more to worry about, not less.

### 6.2 Motivation probe vs capability probe

- **Motivation probe** ("Why this company?") → forward-looking, what-you'd-contribute, anchored in something real and specific about THEM. Never job-need ("I need a job, I'm casting a wide net").
- **Capability probe** ("Have you worked with Y?") → fluency framing: lead with what's possible, bound the rep delta, name how you close gaps.

### 6.3 Mistake question

See Section 2.13. Wants a demonstrated-learning rep, not a defense of the absence of mistakes.

### 6.4 Soft-skill probe (stakeholder communication) — often the highest-stakes pattern

"How do you bring non-technical stakeholders along?" — same four-beat shape as a capability probe: lead with the demonstrated rep, proof with specifics, bound honestly, close forward. NOT "I'm working on it."

Worth knowing: late-stage cycles are routinely decided on cross-team stakeholder-communication signals, not technical ones. The technical bar gets cleared; the question of whether you can communicate across business and technical teams is what decides offers. Treat stakeholder-communication patterns as offer-deciding, not polish.

### 6.5 Team-enablement probe

"How would you raise the team's bar?" → proactive enablement framing (the 30/60/90 lock in Section 2.14), not the passive helper framing.

### 6.6 The casual-cadence trap

Not a probe shape — a cadence trap. Rehearsed locks (departure narrative, comp anchor, conclusion-first delivery) tend to hold under direct, sharp probing and BREAK under relaxed conversational cadence, where descriptor creep and volunteered context sneak back in. The discipline: lead with the conclusion in two sentences, then offer the deep-dive, and let the interviewer pick — even when the conversation feels like peers chatting.
